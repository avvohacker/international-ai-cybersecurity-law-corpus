# The International Law of AI: A Short Source-Verified Account

**Status:** 27 November 2026. **Scope:** the 64 AI-domain instruments recorded in `catalog/instruments/` and counted in [`docs/coverage.md`](coverage.md) [35]. This is a summary of the record, not a claim that the record is complete. Bracketed numbers refer to the citation list at the end; each entry gives the record, the source ID and the official URL. Statements about absence are statements about this corpus and the searches recorded in [`docs/research-log.md`](research-log.md) [31], not assertions that no such source exists anywhere.

**How to cite:** International AI and Cybersecurity Law Corpus, “The International Law of AI: A Short Source-Verified Account,” 27 November 2026, `docs/international-ai-law.md`.

## 1. The short answer

The corpus does not record a universal binding AI treaty in force [1][2][31][35][36]. CETS 225 is not in force; WAICO's entry into force is unconfirmed because the number of deposits is unknown [1][2]; section 2.1 gives their status. At the international level, the binding AI-related content in force is in four digital trade agreements, whose AI clauses are mostly cooperative rather than prescriptive [3]–[6], and in one bilateral ICT agreement, also cooperative [52]; the AfCFTA Protocol's status is unknown, and its annex nonetheless uses “shall” obligations for State Parties once it is in force for them [7]. The general-treaty layer is limited to the ICCPR and the VCLT [8][9]; other general international law, including the UN Charter and international humanitarian law, is a declared scope limit [32]. The only binding horizontal AI regulation in the corpus is the EU AI Act, which is regional law, not international law [10]. Alongside the treaties is a dense layer of non-binding instruments: UN resolutions, recommendations and voluntary codes [11]–[17][37], summit declarations and coalition statements [18]–[28], and technical standards [33]. The recorded institutions do not yet exercise binding decision-making or enforcement [1][2][12][29][30].

## 2. Binding international law

### 2.1 CETS 225 is not in force; WAICO's entry into force is unconfirmed

**CETS 225.** The Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law was adopted on 17 May 2024 and opened for signature on 5 September 2024. The Treaty Office chart of 25 November 2026 records 21 signatures and 2 ratifications: Bosnia and Herzegovina (28 October 2026) and the European Union (15 May 2026). It is not in force. Article 30(3) requires five ratifications, including at least three Council of Europe member States; three more ratifications, at least two by member States, are needed. The Conference of the Parties therefore does not yet exist [1].

**WAICO.** The Agreement on the Establishment of the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization was signed on 16 July 2026. China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs reports 38 founding members; the signing records identify 37: 29 States at the 16 July ceremony and eight later signatories named on 30–31 July 2026. Article 15 requires three instruments of ratification, acceptance or approval. China's National People's Congress approved ratification on 6 November 2026, Nicaragua's Presidency ratified it by decree No. 11-2026 of 16 October 2026 and Russia's Government accepted it by decree No. 1532 of 17 November 2026, but all three are domestic acts, not international deposits. Nicaragua's decree orders the instrument of ratification to be issued for deposit with China, but no deposit was verified. No depositary register or notification was located: the number of deposited instruments is unknown, not zero, and entry into force is therefore unconfirmed rather than negated [2].

Neither treaty is a product-regulation. CETS 225 is a framework convention on human rights, democracy and the rule of law; WAICO establishes an intergovernmental organization with a Council and a Secretariat and limits its functions to the civilian domain [1][2]. The WAICO text read is the official NPC reproduction; the record marks the absence of a separately deposited certified copy as a gap [2].

### 2.2 Agreements with AI provisions: five in force, one unknown

DEPA entered into force on 7 January 2021 for New Zealand and Singapore, on 23 November 2021 for Chile and on 3 May 2024 for Korea [3]. Costa Rica presented its instrument of accession on 17 July 2026; Chile’s SUBREI records the accession as effective on 14 September 2026, and the joint release dated 17 September 2026 welcomes Costa Rica as the fifth Party without stating a date. The New Zealand MFAT overview still says four Parties and is stale [3]. SADEA entered into force on 8 December 2020 between Australia and Singapore; Article 31 is its AI cooperation provision [4]. UKSDEA entered into force on 14 June 2022 between the United Kingdom and Singapore [5]. KSDPA entered into force on 14 January 2023 between Korea and Singapore [6]. SADEA, UKSDEA and KSDPA are bilateral, have two Parties each and record no accession procedure. The AfCFTA Digital Trade Protocol was adopted on 18 February 2024; the AU treaty page displays no entry-into-force date and no ratification table, so its status is unknown, not “not in force” [7].

Outside the trade layer, the Georgia–Uzbekistan Agreement on Cooperation in ICT, Digitalization, Innovations and Post was signed at Tbilisi on 3 July 2026 and entered into force on signature under Article 10 [52]. It is a binding bilateral treaty between Georgia and Uzbekistan. Artificial intelligence is named in the preamble and in Article 2 as an area of cooperation, but the Agreement is not AI-specific and its AI clauses are programmatic; its English text was transcribed and segmented into 10 article units [52].

Most AI clauses in these agreements are cooperative. The AfCFTA Annex on Emerging and Advanced Technologies is different in wording: Article 4 requires State Parties to facilitate deployment and to adopt or maintain laws and regulations and not to deny digital trade solely because a person uses emerging technologies; Article 5 requires national treatment; Article 12 addresses technical standards and risk-based regulation. Those are obligations for State Parties, not a global product rule, and they apply only once the Protocol is in force for the State concerned [7].

A full-text keyword check of the other trade texts concerning Latin America on 26 November 2026 found no occurrence of “artificial intelligence” (or its Spanish and Portuguese equivalents) and no express AI-specific provision in the CPTPP, the EU–Chile ITA and Advanced Framework Agreement, the EU–Mercosur iTA, the Pacific Alliance Additional Protocol, the Pacific Alliance–Singapore FTA, the Mercosur–Singapore FTA or the Mercosur E-Commerce Agreement. They are therefore not counted as AI-provisions instruments. DEPA remains the only Latin American trade agreement in the corpus with an AI clause. A separate bilateral instrument, the Brazil–China memorandum of understanding on AI cooperation of 13 May 2025, is AI-specific and is catalogued as a non-binding interinstitutional act, not a treaty [47][48][49]. Its Parties are China's National Development and Reform Commission and Brazil's Ministry of Development, Industry, Trade and Services, not the two States. The Brazilian Ministry of Foreign Affairs' Concórdia guidance defines an “ato internacional interinstitucional” as an act concluded by a ministry or public entity with its foreign counterpart, not in the name of the Brazilian State, and states that it must always be non-binding and must not create international legal obligations for Brazil [48]. The MRE page on international acts makes the same distinction [49]. Article 6's “Este MdE entra em vigor na data de sua assinatura pelas Partes” fixes the effective date of that administrative cooperation arrangement, not a treaty entry into force; the text as a whole provides for no ratification, deposit or registration [47]. The MOU is therefore not counted among the binding agreements. Other bilateral AI MOUs in the region were not read and are not catalogued or listed.

### 2.3 General international law

The ICCPR entered into force on 23 March 1976 and is a general human-rights treaty, not an AI-specific instrument [8]. The VCLT entered into force on 27 January 1980; the UN Treaty Collection records 45 signatories and 119 parties as of 2 November 2026. It governs treaty law, not AI substance [9].

The corpus does not systematically cover customary international law, general principles of law or *jus cogens*; the research log records no AI-specific Security Council resolution [31][32]. These are scope limits, not findings that such a rule does not exist. International humanitarian law is relevant to military AI, but the corpus contains no adopted binding instrument on autonomous weapons; the CCW GGE's 2026 report contains elements of an instrument, not a treaty [29].

### 2.4 The EU AI Act is regional, not international

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages. It is a regulation of the European Union, not an international treaty; it binds EU Member States and market actors within EU law, not third States as such [10]. The corpus treats it separately from international treaties; national legislation is outside the corpus except this regulation [10][32]. The cyber layer applies the same rule to NIS2 and the Cyber Resilience Act: they are EU internal law, catalogued without a State-level map and therefore shown without a timeline box, not counted as international binding instruments.

## 3. Non-binding instruments

### 3.1 UN resolutions, recommendations and voluntary codes

UNGA resolution 78/265 (21 March 2024) was adopted without a vote and is non-binding [11]. Resolution 79/325 (26 August 2025) establishes the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI and the Global Dialogue on AI Governance; it is non-binding, and its mandate expressly excludes military AI [12]. The Pact for the Future (A/RES/79/1, 22 September 2024) contains the Global Digital Compact, including the decision to establish the Panel and the Dialogue; it is non-binding [13]. The UNGA resolutions on lethal autonomous weapons systems (78/241, 79/62, 80/57) are non-binding and do not themselves prohibit or regulate LAWS; the resolutions on military AI (79/239, 80/58) are also non-binding [14].

The Human Rights Council's resolution 63/15 (6 October 2026) applies international human-rights law across the AI life cycle; it was adopted without a vote and is non-binding [37].

The UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (23 November 2021) is voluntary; UNESCO monitors implementation [15]. The OECD Recommendation on AI (OECD/LEGAL/0449, 22 May 2019) is an in-force OECD legal instrument, not a treaty; it has adherents among OECD members and non-members, and the record notes no sanctions for non-implementation [16]. The G7 Hiroshima Process International Code of Conduct (30 October 2023) is a voluntary code addressed to organizations [17]. The G20 AI Principles are the OECD principles as endorsed by the G20, not a separate text [31].

### 3.2 Summit declarations and coalition statements

Bletchley (1 November 2023): 28 States and the EU at adoption; New Zealand joined on 23 October 2024, and the record lists 29 States and the EU as of 23 November 2026. It is a political endorsement [18]. Seoul (21 May 2024): 10 States and the EU at the Leaders' Session; a political declaration [19]. Paris (11 February 2025): 65 endorsers on the Élysée page (63 States, the EU and the African Union Commission); the PDF lists 64; non-binding [21]. New Delhi (19 February 2026): the PIB annex itemises 92 endorsers as of 5 March 2026 — 90 States, the EU and IFAD; the MEA page lists 91 and omits Colombia; non-binding [22]. BRICS (6 July 2025): a collective leaders' statement by the 11 BRICS member States, with no individual signature block; non-binding [23].

Regional instruments sit outside the summit series. The pan-Arab layer comprises the Unified Arab AI Strategy (launched January 2025) [39], the Arab Charter on AI Ethics (published 2025; exact adoption date not fixed) [40] and the ALECSO Charter on AI Ethics (adopted 17 June 2025) [41]. At the Gulf level, the GCC Guiding Manual on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence Use (version 1.0, November 2023) is non-binding guidance issued by the Ministerial Committee for eGovernment [51]. The Ibero-American layer comprises the CLAD Charter on AI in Public Administration (approved 20 November 2023) [42] and the 2024 Ibero-American Declaration on Ethical and Trustworthy AI, with 18 endorsing States [43]. The military-AI summit sequence comprises the 2023 REAIM Call to Action [44], the 2024 Blueprint for Action (61 States according to the Korean MOFA) [45] and the 2026 Pathways to Action (45 States listed by Spain as of 25 November 2026) [46]. None of these is a binding regional or military-AI treaty; they create no ratification, depositary, monitoring or enforcement mechanism.

GPAI's 2023 Ministerial Declaration (New Delhi, 13 December 2023) is a non-binding ministerial declaration with 14 numbered paragraphs on trustworthy AI, GPAI projects, capacity-building and international cooperation; it is catalogued separately [50]. GPAI itself is a multistakeholder initiative hosted by the OECD, and its Terms of Reference state that membership and participation create no legally binding rights or obligations [50].

Pax Silica (12 December 2025): 26 signatories (25 States and the EU). The official publications call them “signatories,” but they endorse a political declaration; the act is not a treaty signature and creates no ratification, depositary or legal obligation [24]. The San Francisco Declaration on the Secure Foundations of Enduring Prosperity (15 November 2026) is a separate ten-government political declaration, not a treaty [38]. China's Global AI Governance Initiative (18 October 2023) is a unilateral policy initiative with no formal adherence list [25]. The US-led military AI declaration (16 February 2023) has 58 endorsing States as of 27 November 2024; endorsement is political [26]. The UK–US AI Safety MOU (1 April 2024) is a bilateral non-binding MOU; only a summary was published, not the full text [27]. The US–EU TTC joint statement (5 April 2024) records an AI-safety dialogue between the European AI Office and the US AI Safety Institute; no standalone AI-safety agreement was located [28]. No TTC ministerial after September 2024 was located, and the Commission's Digital Dialogues page of 9 October 2026 and its International digital cooperation page of 20 November 2026 did not list a United States dialogue; no launch document for a US–EU Digital Dialogue was located.

### 3.3 What soft law does and does not do

None of these instruments creates a legal obligation, ratification, depositary or enforcement mechanism [18]–[28]. They can inform state practice, national law and standards, but the corpus does not record a finding that any has crystallised into customary law [32].

## 4. Institutions

The UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance and the Independent International Scientific Panel were established by resolution 79/325. The Dialogue's inaugural session was held in Geneva on 6–7 July 2026 with delegations from 163 countries; the 2027 session is scheduled for New York on 3–4 May 2027. The Dialogue issues no binding recommendations [12][30].

WAICO is a treaty-based intergovernmental organization with a Council and a Secretariat; its 37 named members include no G7 economy or EU member State, and the 38th reported member is unnamed [2]. Neither WAICO nor CETS 225 has begun institutional operation: WAICO's organs depend on entry into force, and CETS 225's Conference of the Parties does not yet exist [1][2]. A WAICO promotion meeting on 17 July 2026, convened by the NDRC and MFA, was not a Council plenary and did not constitute organs [2]. The CCW GGE on LAWS has no adopted instrument; its 2026 report contains elements of an instrument, and the Seventh Review Conference continued the mandate to 2028 [29].

The OECD and UNESCO instruments are recommendations, not treaties; the records describe voluntary implementation and no sanctions [15][16]. Pax Silica is recorded as a political declaration; the record notes no treaty, ratification, depositary, monitoring or enforcement [24].

No recorded institution is a universal regulator with binding decisions.

## 5. Gaps and limits

- **WAICO party data.** The 38th founding member is not named; 37 are identified. China's approval, Nicaragua's ratification decree and Russia's acceptance decree are domestic acts, not deposits; no deposit was verified. Bangladesh announced on 1 August 2026 that it would join “initially as an observer”; Article 11 requires an application and Council approval, and no admission is recorded. Singapore has been reported as considering an invitation; it is not a member [2].
- **WAICO text.** The official deposited or certified copy was not acquired; the record reads the official Chinese text published by the National People's Congress, and the English reproduction was consulted only as a cross-check [2].
- **No AI-specific Security Council resolution.** None was located in the searches recorded in the research log [31].
- **Scope limits.** The corpus does not systematically cover customary international law, general principles, *jus cogens*, Security Council resolutions or international humanitarian law [32].
- **Cyber layer.** The 2018 Paris Call for Trust and Security in Cyberspace [20] is catalogued in the separate cyber layer and is outside the 64 AI-domain records summarised here.
- **Autonomous weapons.** No binding instrument has been adopted; the CCW GGE has elements, not a treaty [29].
- **Monitoring and enforcement.** The recorded instruments do not create global monitoring, verification or enforcement; WAICO provides only for consultation (Article 13) [2].
- **Regional law.** The EU AI Act is regional law, not international law [10].
- **Standards.** The ISO/IEC, ITU-T, IEEE and CEN records are non-binding technical standards, not treaties; the corpus does not treat them as binding international law [33]. ITU-T Y.3172 (2019) is in force, IEEE 7000-2021 is an active general systems-engineering standard, and EN 18286:2026 is a published European standard supporting the EU AI Act.
- **Pending candidates.** The International AI Safety Report, the G7 Guiding Principles and Reporting Framework, UNGA 81 drafts L.34/L.37 and the separate Second Committee draft A/C.2/81/L.2/Rev.1 are pending in the candidate register. The two First Committee drafts were approved in October 2026 and the Second Committee draft on 24 November 2026 (79-57-47); all three awaited the plenary and had no A/RES/81 symbol on 26 November 2026. The GPAI 2023 Ministerial Declaration is now catalogued as a non-binding full-text record [50]; GPAI itself is a multistakeholder initiative, not an instrument. ITU-T Y.3172, IEEE 7000-2021 and CEN EN 18286:2026 are now catalogued in the standards layer, and CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 is excluded because a committee is not an instrument. The Council of Europe Recommendation CM/Rec(2020)1, the G20 AI Prosperity Objectives and the G7 Digital and Technology Ministerial Declaration of 29 May 2026 are also catalogued; the companion G20 AI Prosperity Compact is excluded as a private-sector pledge. Candidates are not counted among the 64 AI-domain records unless catalogued [36].

## 6. Analytical reading (not a legal finding)

This section is the account's interpretation, not a legal status. The corpus supports a geopolitical reading of the “three poles” image rather than a legal classification of States: technological competition and regulatory models; forum choice in a regime complex; hedging and overlapping coalitions; and the limits of legal form [34]. The reading is developed in this document and in the atlas's timeline notes; the atlas no longer carries a separate geopolitical essay, and the reading is not a classification of States.

The factual premises cut across the blocs: Kazakhstan appears in Pax Silica and WAICO [2][24]; the EU is in Pax Silica and has ratified CETS 225 [1][24]; the United States signed CETS 225 and Pax Silica but did not endorse Paris [1][21][24]; China endorsed Paris and Bletchley but did not sign CETS 225 or Pax Silica [1][18][21][24].
