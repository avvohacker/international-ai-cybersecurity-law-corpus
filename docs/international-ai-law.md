# The International Law of AI: A Short Source-Verified Account

**Status:** 24 November 2026. **Scope:** the 46 AI-layer instruments recorded in `catalog/instruments/` and counted in [`docs/coverage.md`](coverage.md) [34]. This is a summary of the record, not a claim that the record is complete. Bracketed numbers refer to the citation list at the end; each entry gives the record, the source ID and the official URL. Statements about absence are statements about this corpus and the searches recorded in [`docs/research-log.md`](research-log.md) [30], not assertions that no such source exists anywhere.

**How to cite:** International AI and Cybersecurity Law Corpus, “The International Law of AI: A Short Source-Verified Account,” 24 November 2026, `docs/international-ai-law.md`.

## 1. The short answer

The corpus does not record a universal binding AI treaty in force [1][2][30][34][35]. CETS 225 is not in force; WAICO's entry into force is unconfirmed because the number of deposits is unknown [1][2]; section 2.1 gives their status. At the international level, the binding AI-related content in force is in four digital trade agreements, whose AI clauses are mostly cooperative rather than prescriptive [3]–[6]; the AfCFTA Protocol's status is unknown, and its annex nonetheless uses “shall” obligations for State Parties once it is in force for them [7]. The general-treaty layer is limited to the ICCPR and the VCLT [8][9]; other general international law, including the UN Charter and international humanitarian law, is a declared scope limit [31]. The only binding horizontal AI regulation in the corpus is the EU AI Act, which is regional law, not international law [10]. Alongside the treaties is a dense layer of non-binding instruments: UN resolutions, recommendations and voluntary codes [11]–[17][36], summit declarations and coalition statements [18]–[27], and technical standards [32]. The recorded institutions do not yet exercise binding decision-making or enforcement [1][2][12][28][29].

## 2. Binding international law

### 2.1 CETS 225 is not in force; WAICO's entry into force is unconfirmed

**CETS 225.** The Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law was adopted on 17 May 2024 and opened for signature on 5 September 2024. The Treaty Office chart of 23 November 2026 records 21 signatures and 2 ratifications: Bosnia and Herzegovina (28 October 2026) and the European Union (15 May 2026). It is not in force. Article 30(3) requires five ratifications, including at least three Council of Europe member States; three more ratifications, at least two by member States, are needed. The Conference of the Parties therefore does not yet exist [1].

**WAICO.** The Agreement on the Establishment of the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization was signed on 16 July 2026. China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs reports 38 founding members; the signing records identify 37: 29 States at the 16 July ceremony and eight later signatories named on 30–31 July 2026. Article 15 requires three instruments of ratification, acceptance or approval. China's National People's Congress approved ratification on 6 November 2026, but that is a domestic authorisation, not an international deposit. No depositary register or notification was located: the number of deposited instruments is unknown, not zero, and entry into force is therefore unconfirmed rather than negated [2].

Neither treaty is a product-regulation. CETS 225 is a framework convention on human rights, democracy and the rule of law; WAICO establishes an intergovernmental organization with a Council and a Secretariat and limits its functions to the civilian domain [1][2]. The WAICO text read is the official NPC reproduction; the record marks the absence of a separately deposited certified copy as a gap [2].

### 2.2 Trade agreements with AI provisions: four in force, one unknown

DEPA entered into force on 7 January 2021 for New Zealand and Singapore, on 23 November 2021 for Chile and on 3 May 2024 for Korea [3]. Costa Rica acceded on 17 September 2026 as the fifth Party and the second State to accede after Korea; the joint release does not state its entry-into-force date [3]. SADEA entered into force on 8 December 2020; Article 31 is its AI cooperation provision [4]. UKSDEA entered into force on 14 June 2022 [5]. KSDPA entered into force on 14 January 2023 [6]. The AfCFTA Digital Trade Protocol was adopted on 18 February 2024; the AU treaty page displays no entry-into-force date and no ratification table, so its status is unknown, not “not in force” [7].

Most AI clauses in these agreements are cooperative. The AfCFTA Annex on Emerging and Advanced Technologies is different in wording: Article 4 requires State Parties to facilitate deployment and to adopt or maintain laws and regulations and not to deny digital trade solely because a person uses emerging technologies; Article 5 requires national treatment; Article 12 addresses technical standards and risk-based regulation. Those are obligations for State Parties, not a global product rule, and they apply only once the Protocol is in force for the State concerned [7].

### 2.3 General international law

The ICCPR entered into force on 23 March 1976 and is a general human-rights treaty, not an AI-specific instrument [8]. The VCLT entered into force on 27 January 1980; the UN Treaty Collection records 45 signatories and 119 parties as of 2 November 2026. It governs treaty law, not AI substance [9].

The corpus does not systematically cover customary international law, general principles of law or *jus cogens*; the research log records no AI-specific Security Council resolution [30][31]. These are scope limits, not findings that such a rule does not exist. International humanitarian law is relevant to military AI, but the corpus contains no adopted binding instrument on autonomous weapons; the CCW GGE's 2026 report contains elements of an instrument, not a treaty [28].

### 2.4 The EU AI Act is regional, not international

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages. It is a regulation of the European Union, not an international treaty; it binds EU Member States and market actors within EU law, not third States as such [10]. The corpus treats it separately from international treaties; national legislation is outside the corpus except this regulation [10][31].

## 3. Non-binding instruments

### 3.1 UN resolutions, recommendations and voluntary codes

UNGA resolution 78/265 (21 March 2024) was adopted without a vote and is non-binding [11]. Resolution 79/325 (26 August 2025) establishes the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI and the Global Dialogue on AI Governance; it is non-binding, and its mandate expressly excludes military AI [12]. The Pact for the Future (A/RES/79/1, 22 September 2024) contains the Global Digital Compact, including the decision to establish the Panel and the Dialogue; it is non-binding [13]. The UNGA resolutions on lethal autonomous weapons systems (78/241, 79/62, 80/57) are non-binding and do not themselves prohibit or regulate LAWS; the resolutions on military AI (79/239, 80/58) are also non-binding [14].

The Human Rights Council's resolution 63/15 (6 October 2026) applies international human-rights law across the AI life cycle; it was adopted without a vote and is non-binding [36].

The UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (23 November 2021) is voluntary; UNESCO monitors implementation [15]. The OECD Recommendation on AI (OECD/LEGAL/0449, 22 May 2019) is an in-force OECD legal instrument, not a treaty; it has adherents among OECD members and non-members, and the record notes no sanctions for non-implementation [16]. The G7 Hiroshima Process International Code of Conduct (30 October 2023) is a voluntary code addressed to organizations [17]. The G20 AI Principles are the OECD principles as endorsed by the G20, not a separate text [30].

### 3.2 Summit declarations and coalition statements

Bletchley (1 November 2023): 28 States and the EU at adoption; New Zealand joined on 23 October 2024, and the record lists 29 States and the EU as of 23 November 2026. It is a political endorsement [18]. Seoul (21 May 2024): 10 States and the EU at the Leaders' Session; a political declaration [19]. Paris (11 February 2025): 65 endorsers on the Élysée page (63 States, the EU and the African Union Commission); the PDF lists 64; non-binding [20]. New Delhi (19 February 2026): the PIB annex itemises 92 endorsers as of 5 March 2026 — 90 States, the EU and IFAD; the MEA page lists 91 and omits Colombia; non-binding [21]. BRICS (6 July 2025): a collective leaders' statement by the 11 BRICS member States, with no individual signature block; non-binding [22].

Pax Silica (12 December 2025): 26 signatories (25 States and the EU). The official publications call them “signatories,” but they endorse a political declaration; the act is not a treaty signature and creates no ratification, depositary or legal obligation [23]. China's Global AI Governance Initiative (18 October 2023) is a unilateral policy initiative with no formal adherence list [24]. The US-led military AI declaration (16 February 2023) has 58 endorsing States as of 27 November 2024; endorsement is political [25]. The UK–US AI Safety MOU (1 April 2024) is a bilateral non-binding MOU; only a summary was published, not the full text [26]. The US–EU TTC joint statement (5 April 2024) records an AI-safety dialogue between the European AI Office and the US AI Safety Institute; no standalone AI-safety agreement was located [27].

### 3.3 What soft law does and does not do

None of these instruments creates a legal obligation, ratification, depositary or enforcement mechanism [18]–[27]. They can inform state practice, national law and standards, but the corpus does not record a finding that any has crystallised into customary law [31].

## 4. Institutions

The UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance and the Independent International Scientific Panel were established by resolution 79/325. The Dialogue's inaugural session was held in Geneva on 6–7 July 2026 with delegations from 163 countries; the 2027 session is scheduled for New York on 3–4 May 2027. The Dialogue issues no binding recommendations [12][29].

WAICO is a treaty-based intergovernmental organization with a Council and a Secretariat; its 37 named members include no G7 economy or EU member State, and the 38th reported member is unnamed [2]. Neither WAICO nor CETS 225 has begun institutional operation: WAICO's organs depend on entry into force, and CETS 225's Conference of the Parties does not yet exist [1][2]. The CCW GGE on LAWS has no adopted instrument; its 2026 report contains elements of an instrument, and the Seventh Review Conference continued the mandate to 2028 [28].

The OECD and UNESCO instruments are recommendations, not treaties; the records describe voluntary implementation and no sanctions [15][16]. Pax Silica is recorded as a political declaration; the record notes no treaty, ratification, depositary, monitoring or enforcement [23].

No recorded institution is a universal regulator with binding decisions.

## 5. Gaps and limits

- **WAICO party data.** The 38th founding member is not named; 37 are identified. Bangladesh announced on 1 August 2026 that it would join “initially as an observer”; Article 11 requires an application and Council approval, and no admission is recorded. Singapore has been reported as considering an invitation; it is not a member [2].
- **WAICO text.** The official deposited text was not acquired; the record reads the Agreement from a reproduction [2].
- **No AI-specific Security Council resolution.** None was located in the searches recorded in the research log [30].
- **Scope limits.** The corpus does not systematically cover customary international law, general principles, *jus cogens*, Security Council resolutions or international humanitarian law [31].
- **Autonomous weapons.** No binding instrument has been adopted; the CCW GGE has elements, not a treaty [28].
- **Monitoring and enforcement.** The recorded instruments do not create global monitoring, verification or enforcement; WAICO provides only for consultation (Article 13) [2].
- **Regional law.** The EU AI Act is regional law, not international law [10].
- **Standards.** The ISO/IEC records are non-binding technical standards, not treaties; the corpus does not treat them as binding international law [32].
- **Pending candidates.** The International AI Safety Report, Council of Europe Recommendation CM/Rec(2020)1, ITU-T Y.3172, IEEE 7000, CEN-CENELEC JTC 21, the G7 Guiding Principles and Reporting Framework, the G7 2026 Digital and Technology Ministerial Declaration, the G20 AI Prosperity Compact and Objectives, and UNGA 81 drafts L.34/L.37 are pending in the candidate register and are not counted in the 46 AI-layer records [35].

## 6. Analytical reading (not a legal finding)

This section is the account's interpretation, not a legal status. The corpus supports a geopolitical reading of the “three poles” image rather than a legal classification of States: technological competition and regulatory models; forum choice in a regime complex; hedging and overlapping coalitions; and the limits of legal form [33]. The reading is developed in this document and in the atlas's timeline notes; the atlas no longer carries a separate geopolitical essay, and the reading is not a classification of States.

The factual premises cut across the blocs: Kazakhstan appears in Pax Silica and WAICO [2][23]; the EU is in Pax Silica and has ratified CETS 225 [1][23]; the United States signed CETS 225 and Pax Silica but did not endorse Paris [1][20][23]; China endorsed Paris and Bletchley but did not sign CETS 225 or Pax Silica [1][18][20][23].
