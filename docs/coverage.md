The source-family audit that produced the current additions and exclusions is in [`coverage-checklist.md`](coverage-checklist.md).

## Explicit gaps

1. **Two AI-specific treaties, neither confirmed in force.** CETS 225 and the WAICO Establishment Agreement are catalogued; the corpus does not establish that no others exist, only that no others were located in this cycle. CETS 225 is not in force: it lacks the five ratifications required by Article 30(3). For WAICO, Article 15 requires three instruments, but no depositary register or notification was located: the number of deposits is unknown, not zero, and entry into force is unconfirmed. The source-family checklist found no third AI-specific treaty.
2. **Only one negotiation record.** The CCW GGE on LAWS is catalogued, but no draft protocol or adopted instrument exists; the CoE drafting history and any UN framework-convention proposal were not acquired. UNGA 81 drafts L.34, L.37 and A/C.2/81/L.2/Rev.1 are pending candidates, not adopted instruments.
3. **Military AI** is covered by non-binding resolutions and a political declaration, not by a binding instrument. CETS 225 excludes national defence; the UN Panel/Dialogue exclude military AI.
4. **CETS 225 party data are captured** at the Treaty Office status of 25 November 2026: 21 signatories and 2 ratifications (Bosnia and Herzegovina, 28 October 2026; EU, 15 May 2026). Declarations by the EU, Norway and Ukraine are reproduced; no reservations or objections are recorded. The treaty is not in force.
5. **Languages.** English is the working language of most stored texts, but five primary texts are stored in their authentic languages: the Unified Arab AI Strategy in Arabic, the CLAD Charter and the Ibero-American Declaration in Spanish, the CIS 2018 Agreement in Russian and WAICO in Chinese. The authentic French text of CETS 225 is also stored separately (`text/fr.txt`) and segmented into 36 article units (`units-fr.jsonl`); other official versions are recorded but not acquired, and no translations were produced.
6. **Standards.** Eight technical-standard records are catalogued at metadata level: five ISO/IEC standards and CEN EN 18286:2026, all paywalled, plus ITU-T Y.3172 and IEEE 7000-2021, which are freely accessible but were not acquired. IEEE 7000-2021 is a general systems-engineering standard, not AI-specific. The CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 committee is not an instrument; its other work items (prEN 18228, prEN 18283 and prEN 18285) are not catalogued individually.
7. **National bilateral registries.** No systematic search of national treaty registries (for example the U.S. TIAS, UK treaty series or national gazettes) was completed. A targeted search of official UK, EU and US pages located the UK–US MOU summary and the US–EU TTC commitment, but not a full UK–US MOU text or a standalone US–EU AI-safety agreement.
8. **Regional coverage is uneven.** Latin America and the Middle East now have verified non-binding regional AI instruments (CLAD 2023; Ibero-American Declaration 2024; Unified Arab AI Strategy 2025; Arab Charter 2025; ALECSO Charter 2025), but neither region has a binding regional AI treaty. Central Asia still has no dedicated regional AI instrument in this cycle. The Arab Charter's adoption date is not fixed, and the GCC manual and Arab Model Law were not added.
9. **Trade-agreement exclusions.** The CPTPP exclusion is now based on a full-text keyword check of the official English text (preamble, 30 chapters and the suspension annex), which contains no occurrence of “artificial intelligence”; the English text prevails on divergence. The exclusions for WTO JSI, USMCA and RCEP remain provisional because no full-text AI keyword check was performed. The EU–Chile ITA and AFA, EU–Mercosur iTA, Pacific Alliance Additional Protocol, Pacific Alliance–Singapore FTA, Mercosur–Singapore FTA and Mercosur E-Commerce Agreement were also checked full-text on 26 November 2026 and contained no express AI provision.
10. **Search coverage** was English-language and institutional; multilingual searches were limited to the official language pages encountered. This is not a global exhaustive search.
11. **The CCW record stores the 2026 GGE report, not the Review Conference final document.** The ten-page report is stored in ten page units; the 2027–2028 negotiating text is not yet available.
12. **The Paris Statement's endorser list is inconsistent.** The official Élysée PDF of 19 February 2025 lists 64 entries; the official page accessed on 23 November 2026 lists 65 (Zimbabwe added). A contemporaneous Canadian release listed 60, including South Africa, the United Arab Emirates and the Holy See (listed as “Vatican”), which are absent from the Élysée lists. No withdrawal or correction is documented.

13. **New Delhi's two official lists disagree.** The MEA page updated 21 February 2026 lists 91 endorsers and omits Colombia; the PIB annex states “as of 5th March 2026” and lists 92, including Colombia. The record uses the later PIB list and itemises 90 States, the EU and IFAD. No official explanation for the difference was located.

14. **BRICS has no individual signatures.** The 2025 statement was issued collectively at the Rio de Janeiro summit and has no signature block. The presidency lists 11 member States; the record counts those listed members, not individually evidenced issuers, and records that the Saudi Press Agency described Saudi Arabia as an invited country. It remains a political statement, not a treaty, and is not mapped: a map of the 11-member group would repeat its membership without adding an individual act. The distinction between corpus scope and map scope is set out in the [scope review](methodology.md#10-scope-review-why-two-instruments-were-nearly-missed).

15. **The Human Rights Council layer is now represented, but only by one resolution.** A/HRC/RES/63/15 (6 October 2026) is catalogued as metadata-verified and non-binding. Its follow-up is fixed in the text: a panel at the 65th session on 'Placing human rights at the centre of artificial intelligence: artificial intelligence capacity-building and bridging the artificial intelligence divide', and an OHCHR report on that theme to the 66th session, informed by consultations including at the margins of the Second Global Dialogue on AI Governance in May 2027. Neither had taken place at verification. The Council's earlier AI resolutions (53/29 and 59/11) are recalled in its preamble but not separately catalogued.

16. **Current processes: one catalogued, three pending adoption.** The G7 Digital and Technology Ministerial Declaration (29 May 2026) is now catalogued as a non-binding record, after its AI sections were read in full and its adoption confirmed by the Japanese MIC and the French presidency. UNGA 81 drafts L.34 (military AI) and L.37 (lethal autonomous weapons systems) were approved by the First Committee on 30 October 2026 (171-4-6) and 29 October 2026 (163-5-9) respectively; the separate Panel/Dialogue draft A/C.2/81/L.2/Rev.1 was approved by the Second Committee on 24 November 2026 (79-57-47). All three had no A/RES/81 symbol on 26 November 2026 and remain in the candidate register pending the plenary. The G20 AI Prosperity Objectives (2 September 2026) are catalogued as a non-binding ministerial deliverable; the companion AI Prosperity Compact is a private-sector pledge and is excluded. The Paris Call for AI Safety and Security (9 November 2026) and the broad G20 Innovation Ministerial Statement are also excluded with reasons in the candidate register.
17. **China's initiative has no adherence list.** It is a unilateral non-binding proposal, not an agreed instrument; the record does not attribute it to any State other than China.

18. **The transatlantic AI-safety records are not treaties.** The UK–US MOU is non-binding and its full text was not published; the US–EU commitment is one AI section of a broad TTC joint statement and creates no binding obligation. The institutions named in 2024 have since been renamed (UK AI Security Institute; NIST CAISSI), and the Commission's current TTC page still describes the framework and its working groups but records no meeting after September 2024, and no US Digital Dialogue launch was located as of 20 November 2026, when neither the Digital Dialogues page nor the International digital cooperation page listed the United States. Neither record supports a claim of a binding transatlantic AI-safety regime.
19. **General international law is not comprehensively covered.** The `general_treaty` layer is a deliberately minimal background selection: the VCLT for treaty law and the ICCPR for human rights. The UN Charter, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the ECHR, Convention 108+ and other general instruments are not catalogued; customary international law, general principles, Security Council resolutions (none AI-specific was located) and international humanitarian law are not systematically covered. Their absence is a scope limit, not a finding that they are irrelevant to AI.
20. **WAICO party data are incomplete.** China's MFA reports 38 founding members, but only 37 are individually identified across the official statements; the 38th is recorded as unknown. Bangladesh is not that member: on 1 August 2026 it announced that it would join 'initially as an observer', and Article 11 requires an observer application and Council approval; no admission is recorded. Singapore's MDDI said on 9 September 2026 that it would consider an invitation, without saying whether it is to membership or observer status, and Singapore is not a member. The Government of China is the depositary under Article 18, but no public depositary register, status table or notification was located. China's approval on 6 November 2026, Nicaragua's ratification decree No. 11-2026 of 16 October 2026 and Russia's acceptance by decree No. 1532 of 17 November 2026 are domestic acts, not deposited ratifications; Nicaragua's decree orders the instrument to be issued for deposit, but no deposit was verified. Deposits are recorded as unknown, not zero, and entry into force is unconfirmed. The map therefore shows signatures, not ratifications.

21. **Cybersecurity coverage is broader but still partial.** Fifteen cyber records are catalogued: nine binding treaties, three internal-law acts and three non-binding instruments with official State lists. The five treaties with stable itemised depositary tables are mapped: Budapest (84 Parties), ETS 189 (38 Parties), CETS 224 (52 signatories, not in force), the AU Malabo Convention (21 signatures, 20 Parties, in force since 8 June 2023) and the UN Cybercrime Convention (96 signatories and 3 Parties, not in force). The Arab Convention on IT Offences, the CIS 2018 Dushanbe Agreement, the CIS 2001 Minsk Agreement and the SCO 2009 Information Security Agreement now have stored full texts and citable units; their current depositary tables are unavailable or not stable, so they are not mapped. NIS2, the Cyber Resilience Act and the ECOWAS Cybercrime Directive are internal law: catalogue records without a State-level map, therefore shown without a timeline box and not mapped as treaties. The three non-binding texts are mapped: the Paris Call (81 States and the EU, dated snapshot), the OECD Recommendation on Digital Security of Critical Activities (39 adherents: 38 OECD members and Brazil, in force but non-binding) and the Pall Mall Code (27 supporting States, GOV.UK version updated 23 October 2025, voluntary). The Counter Ransomware Initiative, OSCE, Global Cyber Leadership Initiative and Accra Call were checked and not added because they do not publish a stable, current list of State supporters: the CRI roster is dated 8 May 2024, the OSCE list is a 57-State membership, the GCLI is an agency network and the Accra Call mixes State and non-State endorsers. The UN GGE and OEWG reports are non-binding consensus reports, but their official lists are an appointed 25-expert group and an open-ended membership, not lists of supporters or adherents, so they are not catalogued or mapped here. General information-security cooperation agreements that are neither cybercrime instruments nor cybersecurity regulation (for example the CIS 2013 and CSTO 2017 information-security agreements) are outside this cycle's scope; cyber standards and cooperation channels also remain pending.

## What “coverage” means here

It means: 77 records (62 AI-domain and 15 cybersecurity-only records: nine cyber treaties, three internal-law acts — NIS2, the Cyber Resilience Act and the ECOWAS Cybercrime Directive — and three non-binding instruments — the Paris Call, the OECD Digital Security Recommendation and the Pall Mall Code), each with at least one official source and a declared verification level; 22 instruments with binding form (18 international — 13 in force and 5 not confirmed in force — and four internal law) and 55 non-binding; 62 stored full texts with hashed originals or transcribed text; 61 records with an acquired original (69 files); 17 maps; one category-6 negotiation record; and a candidate register of 47 entries (30 included, 6 pending, 11 excluded) that records both inclusions and exclusions. Cyber standards and cooperation channels remain pending. It does not mean that every international AI instrument has been found.
